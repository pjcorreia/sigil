01 — Fine Facility Services: The Hidden Company

Fine Facility Services is one of Portugal's most prolific government contractors in the cleaning and facility management sector, with 871 government contracts registered on BASE.gov.pt. Yet basic information about the company — who owns it, who manages it — is deliberately concealed from public view.

Directors deliberately hidden.

Manager and shareholder names are absent from all free public sources — an unusual situation for a company with 871 government contracts and €1M share capital. In a country where the commercial registry (RNPC/IRN) provides basic transparency for most companies, Fine Facility's ownership opacity is a deliberate choice, not an accident.

The Fénix Rebrand

Fine Facility previously operated as Fénix Facility Services before rebranding. The name change — from the mythological bird that rises from ashes — raises immediate questions: what needed to be burned and reborn? Company rebranding in the government contracting space is a recognized red flag for entities attempting to distance themselves from past performance issues or investigations.

02 — The Rua Rosa Damasceno Network

Multiple entities linked to Fine Facility share the same registered address: Rua Rosa Damasceno, 11, Lisbon. This pattern — multiple companies at one address, often with shared or hidden directors — is a classic indicator of corporate compartmentalization.

03 — The GNR Scandal: €13.6M Without Written Contracts

Portugal's GNR (Guarda Nacional Republicana — military police) awarded Fine Facility €13.6 million in cleaning contracts between October 2022 and April 2025 via direct adjustment, repeatedly invoking "urgent imperative necessity" to justify bypassing competitive procurement — for three consecutive years.

"Urgent imperative necessity" — for cleaning?

The "urgent necessity" exception in Portuguese procurement law (Código dos Contratos Públicos) is designed for genuine emergencies — natural disasters, security crises, situations where competitive procurement would cause unacceptable delay. Using it repeatedly for three years for cleaning services stretches the concept beyond any reasonable interpretation. As legal experts consulted by media noted: this is "clearly illegal."

The Pattern of Abuse

The "urgent necessity" exception is not unique to GNR. It represents a systemic loophole in Portuguese procurement that allows contracting entities to bypass EU thresholds and competitive requirements by declaring urgency. When the same entity invokes urgency for the same service repeatedly, the urgency is clearly fabricated — the real emergency is the entity's unwillingness to run a competitive tender.

04 — 397 Direct Awards Above Threshold

Across the cleaning and security services sector, SIGIL identified 397 direct awards that exceed EU procurement thresholds — contracts that should have been subject to competitive, EU-wide tender processes but were instead awarded directly to incumbent providers.

EU threshold for services: €143,000 (2024).

Under EU Directive 2014/24/EU, service contracts above €143,000 must be advertised in the Official Journal of the European Union (OJEU) and subject to competitive procurement. Direct awards above this threshold require specific legal justification — technical exclusivity, extreme urgency, or protection of exclusive rights. Cleaning services rarely qualify under any of these exceptions.

05 — The Workers

Behind the corporate structures and procurement loopholes are real people — the cleaning and security workers who operate under these contracts. The outsourcing model that direct awards perpetuate has well-documented consequences:

Low wages, precarious conditions, invisible labor.

Outsourced cleaning and security workers in Portugal's public sector typically earn minimum wage (€870/month in 2026), work irregular hours, and have limited job security. When contracts change hands without competitive bidding, workers lose what little bargaining power they have. The companies bidding for these contracts compete primarily on labor cost — not service quality.

The irony is stark: a procurement system that claims to protect public interest through competitive bidding actually protects incumbents through direct awards — and the workers who deliver the actual services have no voice in either the procurement process or the companies that employ them.

06 — What Nobody Is Talking About

1. Who owns Fine Facility?

A company with 871 government contracts whose directors are deliberately hidden from public registries should trigger immediate investigation. Portugal's RCBE (beneficial ownership register) should contain this information, but access was restricted to "legitimate interest" in October 2025.

2. Is the Fénix network coordinating bids?

Multiple entities at the same address, in the same sector, with some shared management — the structural indicators of bid coordination are present. No competition authority investigation has been announced.

3. How many "urgent necessity" declarations are actually urgent?

A systematic analysis of all "urgent necessity" direct awards on BASE.gov.pt — comparing the claimed urgency against the actual timeline and service type — has never been conducted publicly. The GNR case suggests the exception is being used as a loophole, not an exception.

4. What happened to the workers?

When Fine Facility wins contracts through direct awards rather than competitive tenders, how does this affect employment conditions? Are workers being subcontracted through the shell network? No labor-focused investigation of this sector exists.
