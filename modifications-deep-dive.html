01 — How the Game Works

Contract modification is the least visible but most costly form of procurement manipulation. The pattern is simple: bid low to win the contract, then modify the price upward once competitors have been eliminated. It is legal within limits — and those limits are systematically exploited.

The Tribunal de Contas has flagged "indeterminate concepts" as enabling abuse.

Portugal's Court of Auditors has specifically identified that the vague language used in modification provisions — "change of circumstances," "unforeseen," "public interest" — creates exploitable ambiguity. Contractors and contracting entities can frame almost any cost increase as falling within these broad categories.

02 — The Numbers Tell the Story

The data reveals a clear deterioration in contract price discipline:

Contracts meeting stipulated prices: from 75.3% to 67.1%.

Between 2015–2018 and 2019–2022, the percentage of Portuguese public contracts that were completed at the originally agreed price dropped from 75.3% to 67.1%. By 2022, the contract failure rate reached 32% — meaning roughly one in three public contracts did not perform as agreed.

The "Below Threshold Then Modify Above" Pattern

EU transparency requirements kick in at specific thresholds: €5,538,000 for works contracts and €143,000 for services. A contract awarded at €5 million avoids EU-wide advertising. A 15% modification brings it to €5.75 million — above the threshold but already committed. The initial award price was chosen to avoid scrutiny; the modification captures the true intended price.

Multiple successive modifications are assessed cumulatively — in theory.

EU law requires that successive modifications be assessed against the original contract value. In practice, Portuguese contracting entities often treat each modification as independent, allowing staged increases that individually fall within safe harbors but cumulatively far exceed them.

03 — Sectors Most Affected

Construction & Infrastructure

The construction sector is the most modification-prone, with material cost inflation providing convenient cover. Steel prices rose 45% and timber up to 60% during 2021–2022, giving contractors a legitimate basis for price increase claims. The problem: distinguishing genuine cost escalation from opportunistic price inflation when both use the same legal framework.

Fixed-price contracts from pre-inflation periods.

Contracts signed in 2019–2020 at fixed prices are now being modified upward by 20–40%, with contractors citing material and labor cost increases. Some contractors have gone bankrupt; others have used the modification mechanism to capture windfall adjustments that exceed their actual cost increases.

Healthcare PPPs

Hospital Public-Private Partnerships in Portugal have a documented history of renegotiations that disadvantage the state. Despite this track record, 5 new hospital PPPs were approved — creating future modification risk on contracts worth hundreds of millions of euros.

IT Services

IT contracts are particularly susceptible to modification through scope creep. An initial contract for a "basic system" is modified to include additional modules, integrations, and support — each modification individually justifiable, but collectively transforming a modest contract into a major expenditure without competitive re-tendering.

04 — The "Imprevisão" Loophole

Article 437 of the Portuguese Civil Code allows contract revision when performance becomes "excessively onerous" due to circumstances that were not foreseeable at the time of contracting. This doctrine — imprevisão — is the nuclear option for contract modification.

No fixed limit. Subjective test. Contractor-initiated.

Unlike the statutory modification limits (10–25%), imprevisão has no cap. The test is whether circumstances changed in a way that makes performance "excessively onerous" — a subjective assessment that different judges and arbitrators may evaluate differently. The contractor initiates the claim, placing the burden on the public entity to either accept the modification or face potential contract termination and costly re-procurement.

The distinction between genuine imprevisão (a pandemic, a war disrupting supply chains) and ordinary business risk (material price fluctuations, labor market changes) is the critical boundary. Portuguese law does not draw this line clearly, creating systematic opportunity for contractors to reclassify normal risks as unforeseen events.

06 — What Nobody Is Talking About

1. No systematic modification tracking.

BASE.gov.pt does not consistently track contract modifications or their cumulative impact. It is currently impossible to answer the basic question: how much did Portuguese public contracts cost after modifications versus their award price?

2. The "abnormally low bid" detection gap.

Bids designed to win low and modify high are, by definition, abnormally low at the award stage. Yet Portugal has no systematic method for detecting abnormally low bids — the very tool that would flag modification-prone contracts before they're awarded.

3. Cumulative modification impact on national budget.

No Portuguese institution has published an estimate of the aggregate cost of contract modifications to the public purse. With a 33% contract failure rate and declining price discipline, the number is likely in the hundreds of millions annually.

4. Contractor accountability for failed bids.

When a contractor bids low and modifies high, there are no consequences — the public entity absorbs the cost difference. In other EU countries, repeated modification patterns trigger debarment reviews. In Portugal, the same contractors continue to win new contracts.
