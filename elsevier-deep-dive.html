Elsevier / FCT: The €120M+ Academic Monopoly

01 — The B-On Consortium

B-On (Biblioteca do Conhecimento Online) was created in 2004 as Portugal's national consortium for academic publishing access. It serves 64 institutions — universities, polytechnics, research centers — giving approximately 380,000 users access to scientific journals. It is managed by FCCN (Fundação para a Computação Científica Nacional), itself under FCT.

The model sounds reasonable: pool purchasing power to negotiate better deals. In practice, it created a single negotiating counterpart for publishers — and Elsevier captured it completely. When the entire country negotiates through one entity, there is no competition. There is only the question of how much you will pay.

02 — The Profit Machine

Elsevier (owned by RELX Group, traded on the London Stock Exchange) is the world's largest academic publisher, controlling approximately 16% of all scientific articles. Its business model is uniquely profitable: researchers produce content for free, peer reviewers evaluate it for free, and universities pay to read it.

Profit margin: 37–38%.

Elsevier's operating profit margin exceeds that of Apple, Google, and Amazon. This margin is extracted from publicly funded research — taxpayers pay for the science, then pay again to read it.

The "Big Five" academic publishers (Elsevier, Springer Nature, Wiley, Taylor & Francis, SAGE) control 75% of European journal spending according to a Chemistry World analysis. An antitrust lawsuit was filed against these publishers in September 2024.

05 — The Open Access Alternative

The money Portugal spends on Elsevier subscriptions could fund an entirely different model:

Plan S & Diamond Open Access.

Plan S (launched 2018 by European research funders) mandates that publicly funded research must be freely accessible. Diamond Open Access — where neither authors nor readers pay — is supported by institutions like CERN (SCOAP3 model). Portugal adopted Plan S-aligned policies in February 2025 but simultaneously renewed with Elsevier, creating a contradiction.

Opportunity cost: €15–45M over 15 years.

The estimated Elsevier spend over 15 years could have funded: 150–450 PhD scholarships, 30–90 research lab upgrades, or the development of a national open-access publishing infrastructure. Instead, it generated 37% profit margins for a Dutch corporation.

06 — What Nobody Is Talking About

1. No competitive procurement.

There is no evidence that FCT/FCCN conducted competitive bidding for journal access services. In a market with alternatives (open access, preprint servers, direct publisher agreements), sole-source procurement of this scale warrants scrutiny.

2. NDA-protected public spending.

Public institutions hiding the cost of publicly funded services behind NDAs is antithetical to transparency. No Portuguese journalist, MP, or auditor has publicly challenged this practice.

3. The revolving door.

Whether FCT or FCCN leadership has connections to the publishing industry has never been investigated. The "legitimate interest" restriction on Portugal's beneficial ownership register (RCBE), tightened in October 2025, makes such investigations harder.

4. Researcher lock-in.

Portugal's academic evaluation system (via FCT) rewards publication in high-impact journals — which are overwhelmingly controlled by the Big Five. This creates demand-side lock-in: researchers must publish in Elsevier journals to advance their careers, and then the state must pay Elsevier for access.
