01 — The Vendor Lock-In Pattern

Railway signaling and control systems represent one of the most lock-in-prone sectors in public procurement. Once a railway operator installs a proprietary signaling system, switching vendors requires replacing the entire infrastructure — signals, interlockings, train control software, communication systems — at costs that can exceed the original installation by orders of magnitude.

Siemens Mobility is one of three dominant players globally (alongside Alstom and Hitachi Rail). In Portugal, Siemens has established itself as the primary signaling provider for Infraestruturas de Portugal (IP), the state-owned company managing the national railway and road network.

109 direct awards is not a procurement pattern. It's a dependency.

When the same vendor receives over 100 contracts without competitive bidding from a single state entity, the question shifts from "is there a problem?" to "how did the dependency form, and who benefits from maintaining it?" Direct awards for proprietary system maintenance are legally defensible. But 109 of them suggest a system designed from the start to exclude competition.

02 — The €1.6 Billion Global Corruption Record

In 2008, Siemens AG agreed to pay €1.6 billion in fines — the largest corporate corruption settlement in history at the time — to resolve bribery charges brought by US and German authorities. The company admitted to systematic bribery across multiple countries and business divisions.

Internal investigations revealed that Siemens maintained an organized system for paying bribes across its business divisions, including dedicated slush funds, offshore accounts, and intermediaries. The company's own compliance review identified approximately €1.3 billion in suspicious payments made between 2000 and 2006.

03 — Director Interlocks

Siemens in Portugal operates through multiple subsidiary entities. Key directors hold positions across several of these entities simultaneously, creating information-sharing and coordination capacity that individual subsidiaries nominally competing for contracts should not have.

The revolving door.

Alexandre Fonseca's trajectory — from CEO of Portugal's largest telco (Altice/MEO) to Siemens board member to government advisory roles — exemplifies the kind of cross-sector relationship network that enables preferential access to procurement decisions. His case was documented in Operation Picoas, where he was charged in connection with a scheme involving false invoices worth €380,000+.

04 — How Other Countries Handle This

The vendor lock-in problem in railway infrastructure is not unique to Portugal. What differs is how countries address it:

CountryApproachResult
Germany (DB Netz)Open standards (ETCS) + multi-vendor framework agreementsMultiple suppliers per corridor; vendor switching possible
UK (Network Rail)Modular procurement; separate signaling from installationCompetitive tension between Siemens, Alstom, Hitachi
Netherlands (ProRail)Technology-neutral specifications + market consultationRegular competitive tenders even for legacy systems
Portugal (IP)Proprietary systems + direct awards109 non-competitive contracts to single vendor
ERTMS/ETCS: the standard Portugal should be using.

The European Rail Traffic Management System (ERTMS) was specifically designed to break vendor lock-in. By mandating interoperable standards, EU railway policy aims to create competitive markets. Portugal's continued reliance on proprietary Siemens systems — rather than transitioning to open standards — perpetuates the dependency.

05 — What Nobody Is Talking About

1. No Portuguese media has investigated the IP-Siemens relationship.

Despite 109 direct awards and Siemens' documented global corruption history, no Portuguese outlet has examined whether the procurement pattern at IP represents value for money, vendor lock-in, or something else entirely.

2. Total lifetime value unknown.

The cumulative value of all Siemens contracts with IP has not been publicly calculated. Individual direct awards may appear small, but 109 of them could total hundreds of millions.

3. The initial system selection was never scrutinized.

Every direct award today is justified by the proprietary system already in place. But who chose that system? When? Through what process? If the original selection was influenced, every subsequent direct award inherits that taint.

4. ERTMS transition timeline unclear.

Portugal is nominally committed to ERTMS deployment under EU directives. But the timeline for transitioning from proprietary Siemens systems to interoperable standards — which would break the lock-in — has not been publicly communicated or audited.
