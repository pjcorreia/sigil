01 — Director Interlocks: Legal But Dangerous

Portuguese law does not explicitly prohibit directors from sitting on the boards of multiple companies — even companies that bid on the same government contracts. This creates a structural vulnerability: individuals with fiduciary duties to multiple entities can share information, coordinate strategies, and align pricing across nominally independent competitors.

No prohibition. No disclosure requirement. No monitoring.

Unlike the UK (where the Competition and Markets Authority actively monitors director interlocks) or the US (where Section 8 of the Clayton Act prohibits interlocking directorates between competing companies above certain thresholds), Portugal has no regulatory framework addressing this risk. Companies bidding on government contracts can share directors — and no one is checking.

02 — Operation Picoas: The €100M+ Scheme

Operation Picoas documented a major corruption scheme involving an estimated 60–100 companies. The investigation revealed how corporate networks were used to generate false invoices and funnel public money through shell structures.

Alexandre Fonseca: €380,000+ in false invoices.

The former CEO of Altice/MEO (Portugal's largest telco) and Siemens board member was charged after receiving €380,000+ through false invoicing schemes. The case exemplifies the revolving door between major corporations, government advisory positions, and procurement-adjacent roles.

The investigation alleged fraud exceeding €100 million affecting government procurement, with the network of companies used to create the appearance of legitimate commercial transactions while actually serving as conduits for corrupt payments.

03 — Operation Ambrosia: The 102-Company Network

Operation Ambrosia revealed a network of 102 companies — many with minimal capitalization — used to facilitate procurement fraud. The investigation demonstrated how easy it is to create shell companies in Portugal and use them to win government contracts, subcontract between network entities, and extract public money.

Low capital requirements enable shell creation.

Portuguese company formation requires minimal capital (as low as €1 for some entity types). Combined with the absence of automated network analysis tools in the commercial registry, this creates an environment where shell company networks can proliferate undetected. The 102 companies in Operation Ambrosia were identified only through lengthy criminal investigation — not through routine regulatory monitoring.

04 — The Beneficial Ownership Black Hole

In October 2025, Portugal shifted its RCBE (Registo Central do Beneficiário Efetivo — Central Beneficial Ownership Register) from public access to "legitimate interest" restricted access. This means journalists, researchers, and civil society organizations can no longer freely query who ultimately owns Portuguese companies.

A step backward in transparency — at the worst possible time.

At a time when SIGIL's analysis reveals widespread use of shell structures, hidden directors, and corporate network patterns in public procurement, Portugal made it harder — not easier — to investigate corporate ownership. The restriction was implemented following an EU Court of Justice ruling (C-37/20 and C-601/20) that found unlimited public access disproportionate — but Portugal's implementation appears to go further than required.

07 — What Nobody Is Talking About

1. No automated network detection.

Portugal has no system that automatically identifies when multiple companies bidding on the same contract share directors, addresses, or beneficial owners. Every cartel detection requires manual investigation — reactive, slow, and expensive.

2. The RCBE restriction helps the wrong people.

Restricting beneficial ownership access protects shell company operators more than it protects privacy. Legitimate businesses have nothing to hide from public scrutiny; entities using opaque structures to win government contracts have everything to hide.

3. Director interlock monitoring doesn't exist.

No Portuguese institution monitors whether directors of companies bidding on the same government contracts share board seats. This information exists in commercial registries but is never cross-referenced against procurement data.

4. Portuguese investigative journalism is fragile.

Outlets like Divergente and Fumaça do important work but face economic fragility. Portuguese institutions exhibit what journalists have described as "surreal" opacity in Freedom of Information requests. The infrastructure for accountability — both journalistic and institutional — is itself under-resourced.
